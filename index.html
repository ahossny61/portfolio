How I run a sprint
  1. Groom ahead. While sprint N runs, I help the analysts prepare sprint N+1: story preparation, impact, business gaps, and whether an integration should consume or expose an API.
  2. Definition of Ready gate. At planning, only ready stories enter. I check that developers and QA understand the goal, the implementation steps, and the test cases.
  3. Estimate and review. The team estimates, I review outliers (why so high, why so low), and we agree an average. UI/UX questions are settled against Figma.
  4. Allocate by capacity. I take the team's real capacity, pull stories up to it, and assign them by skill and business background.
  5. Support during the sprint. Developers raise questions on the story, we resolve them with the analysts, and I guide by asking how they would approach it before giving the answer. Database and module design goes through the Technical Manager.
  6. Triage bugs. I check each bug: business gap, regression, enhancement, or a real defect. I set priority; QA sets severity. Rejected bugs go back to QA, valid ones go to the right owner (development, analysis, UI, or UX).
  7. Quality gate. A story with no open bugs is ready to release. At the end of the sprint I report per story and decide with the analysts whether a minor open bug blocks UAT.
  8. UAT and release. I prepare the deployment plan (applications, site properties, timers, configuration) for the DevOps role, support the UAT week alongside the current sprint, release to production on Thursday, and stay online with the operations team through the weekend.

Review on every story: code, architecture and best practices, clean code, and AI Mentor findings.

Refactoring and domain isolation
  • Formed a refactoring team with the other team leads for three months and inventoried technical debt: architecture, performance, security, and maintainability.
  • On OutSystems 11, a release pulled in every application it depended on. We grouped modules and applications into business domains per portfolio and replaced strong dependencies with weak ones through Service Actions, so one portfolio can ship on its own.
  • Used Site Properties as feature toggles so a feature can ship disabled.
  • Tuned queries slower than 200 ms: removed joins and selected columns that were not needed, set limits and pagination, and redesigned queries, sometimes with AI help.
  • Result: roughly 30–40% of the refactoring plan in three months, and about 40–50% of the technical debt reported by AI Mentor cleared.
Automation and AI in delivery
  • Release tickets. Saves about 5 hours a week. A C# scheduled task reads the developers' Excel sheet, resolves applications from LifeTime or the database, and creates the release or deployment ticket in Jira with applications, site properties, and timers.
  • Daily management report. Hours logged, bugs fixed, stories finished, and AI Mentor findings per developer, in a sheet and email. A scheduled AI agent gathers details from Teams and a .NET script builds the report; the plan is to make it fully scripted.
  • AI with OutSystems. Connected an AI agent through MCP so it creates sub-tasks and notifications that follow our patterns, shared blocks, actions, and naming. Stories are pre-validated by AI against the Definition of Ready before the human review.
Working across teams
  • Meetings with external bodies and other portfolios to split ownership of shared features, with their project managers, technical managers, team leads, and analysts.
  • Integrations with the CRM, the ERP (finance), and government systems, all over REST.
  • Environments are Dev, QA, UAT, and Production. Regular reports and ad-hoc requests for the portfolio manager and technical manager; sprint retrospectives and SOS meetings.
  • Proposed centralized logging, an API gateway, and single sign-on to management; they are awaiting approval.

Following the team lead's direction, I ran the migration as Hangfire background jobs in .NET, so the work happened outside user requests. I connected the site to OnBase for file management, added watermarking, and organized the files.

A production incident, contained in a day

Production support, Infath
Situation
A new process, released without testing and without a feature toggle, ran for a day and changed the state of thousands of records across hundreds of projects. Downstream integrations with an external government body were affected.
Action
Stopped the system to prevent more damage, traced the process and the tables it changed, backed those tables up, and rebuilt the earlier state of every affected record. Coordinated corrections with the external body and closed the related requests. A full rollback of code and database was possible but would have lost the data entered after the release, so I chose a targeted restore.
Result
Service resumed the same day with data consistent.
Change
New processes now ship behind a feature toggle and pass a release gate.

A ranking that kept reshuffling

Assignment engine, Infath
Situation
Beneficiaries were ranked for assignment by one qualification date shared by all categories. Every change of qualification pushed a beneficiary to the end of the ranking, and the changes kept coming, so complaints were frequent.
Action
I proposed and helped deliver a qualification date per category. A downgrade to category B no longer moves the beneficiary within B, and an upgrade to A places them last in A only, without touching B.
Result
Stable, explainable rankings and fewer complaints. The same approach fixed several other gaps in the assignment engines.

Large features on short notice

Delivery, Infath
Situation
Big features arrived late, sometimes mid-sprint, with fixed dates.
Action
I raise the schedule risk first. Once the decision stands, I rebalance the sprint, push the team to deliver, and protect performance and code review.
Result
About 30 two-week sprints delivered; two slipped, and I can name the causes.
